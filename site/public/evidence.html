The evidence, honestly

Fianais — what one classroom trial actually showed

Scotling grows out of a 2026 dissertation, User-Centred Design of Games for Gaelic Digital Normalisation (Luke Campbell Macleod). One pilot has been run. Here is exactly what it found, with page references, and — just as importantly — what it does not show.

Internal draft, 16 October 2026, reading the dissertation's methodology and evaluation chapters directly (not an AI summary — this project found that PDF/document summarisers can hallucinate tables, so every figure below was read from the dissertation's own text).

Pilot 1, as run

Three mini-games were trialled with 18 P6–P7 pupils (100% of the present class, one pupil absent) in Gaelic-medium education at Bun-sgoil Steòrnabhaigh, a Western Isles primary, with one teacher supervising and separately completing a post-session questionnaire. The pupil discussion group ran fully in Gaelic; questionnaires were in English. The session took place in the pupils' own classroom, on school devices, during a standard school afternoon (dissertation, p.27).

What was not expected: the intergenerational finding

The discussion group surfaced something the design had not anticipated. Several children described an uneven Gaelic relationship with their grandparents, and — unprompted — suggested the game could help repair it. One participant described it as a potential "Gaelic connection at home." Two children separately said they wanted to use the game to teach Gaelic to parents who do not speak it, reversing the usual direction of transmission. This echoes Fishman's (1991) account of Gaelic surviving through "intergenerational and demographically concentrated home-family-neighbourhood" transmission — a pathway the game's design never set out to test (dissertation, p.66).

Where the evidence runs out

In the dissertation's own words: "While n=18 provides substantial data relative to the target population, it cannot claim generalisability across the broader GME community, particularly in urban settings" (p.67). The discussion group relied on field notes rather than full transcription. Only 2 of 18 participants engaged with the in-game idiom ("seanfhacal") help buttons. The trial teacher separately flagged real gaps — dyslexia-friendly fonts, audio narration variety, adaptive difficulty — that the evaluated build did not yet have (p.66–67). We are not hiding any of this from a school considering Scotling; see For schools for how we are responding to it.

What this page is not

One classroom, one teacher, 18 pupils. Not a controlled trial, not peer-reviewed, not evidence that Scotling works for every GME setting, age band or dialect area. Pilot 2 — with proper parental consent and each child's own ongoing assent, following BERA's 2024 ethical guidelines — has not happened yet.
